# Varman kings through Supratishthitavarman

Research and illustration dossier · 17 October 2026 · version 1

Scope: twelve rulers of Kamarupa, Pushyavarman through Supratishthitavarman inclusive. Bhaskaravarman is used as the issuer of genealogical evidence and as the next ruler, not included as a thirteenth character here. No new character artwork is generated in this dossier.

## How to read this dossier

**Recorded** means the source says it; it does not mean every royal claim is independently proven. **Interpretation** means a historian’s identification or reconstruction. **Design** means an artistic choice, not recovered historical appearance.

The best directly checked evidence for the complete sequence is the Nidhanpur charter, a seventh-century document that looks back on earlier generations. Its poetry is a royal panegyric: metaphors about the moon, gods, generosity and invincibility cannot be converted into literal appearance or lists of documented campaigns.

The translation in Epigraphia Indica XII, printed pp. 76–78, has now been checked against local scan images. Relevant pages are saved in `source_excerpts/`. This updates the earlier pack, where the volume had only been downloaded. The older edition contains gaps later supplemented by additional plates; it is not a substitute for Sharma’s complete modern epigraphic edition.

## Succession and family links

Use **Varman** as the standard English catalogue spelling; retain **Barman** as the user’s familiar spelling. Use Balavarman I to distinguish him from later rulers of that name. Supratisthitavarman and Supratishthitavarman are spelling variants, not separate kings.

These queen names are aligned to their husbands, not shifted one generation upward. The inscription explicitly identifies Bhaskaravarman as Supratishthitavarman’s younger brother, also born of Syamadevi. He is not Supratishthitavarman’s son.

## Dates: do not hard-code the popular table

A widely circulated modern table gives 350–374, 374–398, 398–422, 422–446, 446–470, 470–494, 494–518, 518–542, 542–566, 566–590, 590–595 and 595–600 CE respectively. These are **reconstructed ranges**, not twelve sets of independently attested accession and death dates. Repeating them without “circa” would create false certainty.

There is a concrete warning: an older published report of Bhutivarman’s Barganga inscription reads Gupta year 234 as 554 CE (S. K. Bhuyan, Introduction to Assam, printed p. 25; discovery passage found in IGNCA’s hosted scan). That conflicts with the popular 542 end date. The original inscription edition and era reading require checking before adopting a revised exact chronology. For artwork, retain broad centuries and sequence. Do not silently combine mutually incompatible date systems.

## Individual research cards

### 1. Pushyavarman

Recorded: starts the historical royal sequence in Nidhanpur v. 7. The preceding Naraka–Bhagadatta–Vajradatta story and thousands of years of ancestors belong to dynastic legitimation. The founder is generally situated in the fourth century in scholarship.

Limits: no individual physical description or personal sect is established here. A direct friendship with Samudragupta or a specific battle must not be inferred simply from contemporaneity. The approved character face and ivory/ochre court dress remain artistic reconstruction.

### 2. Samudravarman

Recorded: Pushyavarman’s son, praised through an ocean metaphor and royal prowess (v. 8). Dattadevi is the mother of his son Balavarman (v. 9).

Limits: shared names with Gupta royalty do not demonstrate a marriage alliance, common family or direct naming intention. The moustache, face, terracotta drape and physique in our first sheet are design decisions.

### 3. Balavarman I

Recorded: son of Samudravarman and Dattadevi. Verse 9 praises military strength and armour using wordplay on his name; Ratnavati is the mother of Kalyanavarman.

Limits: this is not an equipment inventory. It does not establish a metal breastplate, helmet shape, mail shirt or named conquest. Do not conflate him with later Balavarmans or automatically with every similarly named ruler in Gupta records.

### 4. Kalyanavarman

Recorded: son of Balavarman and Ratnavati; father of Ganapativarman through Gandharvavati. The charter offers idealising praise rather than a detailed political narrative.

Research issue: conquest of Davaka and identification with a ruler associated with a mission to China are historical reconstructions encountered in P. C. Choudhury’s discussion, not explicit statements of Nidhanpur v. 10. Date, identification and rival explanations need full passage verification. Do not give the character a “conqueror of Davaka, 428 CE” caption yet.

### 5. Ganapativarman

Recorded: son of Kalyanavarman and Gandharvavati; father of Mahendravarman through Yajnavati. Verse 11 stresses giving and qualities through a comparison with Ganapati.

Research issue: Ganendravarman is reported as a Dubi variant. Abdication in favour of his son is reported in historical discussions of Dubi; full Dubi passage has not been checked in this round. The divine comparison does not establish exclusive worship of Ganesha or justify an elephant-faced crown.

### 6. Mahendravarman

Recorded: son of Ganapativarman and Yajnavati; father of Narayanavarman through Suvrata. Nidhanpur v. 12 strongly emphasises sacrificial rites.

Interpretation: historians credit him with two ashvamedhas on the basis of the wider epigraphic record. This supports Brahmanical ritual kingship, not a complete sect biography. Identification with Surendravarman of the Umachal inscription is a scholarly proposal: the names are synonymous names of Indra, but that alone is not conclusive. Keep the Balabhadra shrine and Mahendravarman link conditional until the inscription edition is read.

### 7. Narayanavarman

Recorded: son of Mahendravarman and Suvrata; father of Bhutivarman through Devavati. Verse 13 compares his understanding of the Self with Janaka.

Limits: neither the name Narayana nor this philosophical praise proves exclusive Vaishnava adherence. Claims of a specific ashvamedha, campaign or administrative reform need their own source passage. Do not depict him in monk’s robes because the verse praises philosophy.

### 8. Bhutivarman / Mahabhutavarman

Recorded: son of Narayanavarman and Devavati; father of Chandramukhavarman through Vijnanavati. The later Nidhanpur renewal records an earlier land grant attributed to him. This gives a more concrete administrative context than praise alone.

Religion: the reported Barganga title parama-bhagavata is evidence pointing toward a Vaishnava royal affiliation; distinguish this from the modern assumption that every Varman king had the same affiliation. Source edition/era verification is still needed. The earlier charter concerns Brahman beneficiaries, but that does not tell us every subject’s religion.

Limits: locate Chandrapuri with care; modern scholarship often places it in the Sylhet region, while older histories offered other identifications. Do not turn a disputed location into a hard territorial map. The name variant does not identify a different king.

### 9. Chandramukhavarman

Recorded: son of Bhutivarman and Vijnanavati; father of Sthitavarman through Bhogavati. Verse 15 uses the moon’s digits and the arts as a literary comparison.

Limits: not proof of a pale complexion, a round face, a crescent crown or patronage of a named school of art. A short reign or abdication appears in derivative accounts; do not give exact duration without a checked passage. Personal religious sect remains unresolved here.

### 10. Sthitavarman

Recorded: son of Chandramukhavarman and Bhogavati; father of Susthitavarman through Nayanadevi. Nidhanpur’s comparison with the serpent supporting the earth is poetic, not an image of his regalia.

Interpretation: historical reconstructions credit him with two horse sacrifices and successes against western opponents using Dubi and related evidence. Exact enemies, battle dates and any claim about founding/moving a capital require the underlying passages; they should not be treated as established from Nidhanpur alone.

Religion: reported Vedic sacrifices suggest Brahmanical royal ritual. They do not dictate a Shaiva or Vaishnava forehead mark. No cobra crown follows from the literary metaphor.

### 11. Susthitavarman / Sri-Mriganka

Recorded: son of Sthitavarman and Nayanadevi; father of Supratishthitavarman and Bhaskaravarman through Syamadevi. Nidhanpur explicitly identifies Sri-Mriganka with him and praises giving (vv. 18–19).

Interpretation: Mahasenagupta’s defeat of a Susthitavarman near Lauhitya is connected with this king through the Aphsad record and historical identification. This deserves an independent inscription check. Dubi is reported to describe a political crisis involving his sons; assigning a precise captor, battle, death in combat or release story goes beyond the checked Nidhanpur text.

Limits: distinguish Sthita from Susthita. Mriganka is a royal epithet, not documentary proof of a crescent ornament. Do not claim that a defeated king necessarily wore battle scars or had a particular temperament.

### 12. Supratishthitavarman

Recorded: son of Susthitavarman and Syamadevi; elder brother of Bhaskaravarman. Verse 21 associates him with elephants, learned attendants and a firmly established capital in a mountain simile. His place immediately before Bhaskaravarman is secure within the received genealogy.

Limits: “introduced many reforms”, “built the army inherited by Bhaskaravarman”, exact reign length and claims that he died during his father’s lifetime are not established by the checked passage. Some popular summaries contradict their own succession tables. Keep these unresolved rather than choosing whichever makes a stronger story. No authenticated face, age at accession or personal sect has been found here.

## Religion across the set

The court’s Sanskrit genealogies, sacrificial language and land grants show Brahmanical frameworks; this does not erase local religious traditions or prove uniform belief throughout the kingdom. The Nidhanpur invocation addresses Shiva, while its ancestry uses Vishnu and its metaphors invoke other deities. A charter’s invocation cannot automatically assign the same personal sect to all twelve ancestors.

No checked evidence in this dossier establishes a Buddhist Varman king among these twelve. This is a statement about the present evidence, not proof that Buddhism was absent. Xuanzang’s account concerns the later Bhaskaravarman: his respect for learned Buddhist monks must not be mistaken for personal conversion or projected backward onto Pushyavarman. Later tantric traditions are not automatic fourth-century evidence.

For character sheets: leave sect-specific forehead marks and deity pendants unassigned unless a ruler-specific source justifies them. That is a production choice to manage uncertainty, not a claim that the king had no religious affiliation.
