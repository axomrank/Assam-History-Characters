# Attire research and character plan

17 October 2026. Applies to all twelve kings through Supratishthitavarman.

## Evidence hierarchy

1. **Ruler-specific surviving dress or authenticated portrait:** none established in the consulted material. No complete costume can be certified for any of these kings.
2. **Local, dated archaeological comparison:** Da Parbatia, Tezpur. ASI assigns its Gupta-related doorframe to approximately the sixth century. Its carved figures are religious images, including female river deities; they are not portraits of Varman kings. Use for regional sculptural treatment of cloth and ornament, not a direct male court uniform.
3. **Contemporary comparison outside Kamarupa:** Gupta royal coins and fifth-century sculpture. These help establish a plausible period vocabulary, with geographic and iconographic limits.
4. **Production interpretation:** palette, exact textile weave, gold embroidery, clasp shape, necklace arrangement, hairstyles, facial hair and a separate outfit for each king. These remain design choices.

## Consulted visual references

**R1: Skandagupta gold coin, British Museum 1910,0403.69.** Catalogue dates it about 456–467 CE and describes the king in a waistcloth and jewellery, with a bow. This is useful near the fifth-century portion of our set. It is a Gupta image, not Varman evidence; the ritual/political image may idealise royal dress. The catalogue description was available through indexed text; direct page access returned 403 and no coin photograph was downloaded or visually verified. Do not invent specific border patterns from this record.

**R2: Male Figure, Met 1982.471.1, object 38394.** Museum dates this terracotta to approximately the fifth century. Downloaded public-domain image has been inspected. The surviving upper body is mostly uncovered; the head has elaborate wrapped or arranged head treatment and hair, and an earring is visible. The lower body is missing, so it cannot validate a dhoti cut, girdle or footwear. The catalogue identifies an Indian male figure, not a king of Kamarupa. Avoid using its face as an ethnic template.

**R3: Da Parbatia doorframe, ASI Guwahati Circle.** Downloaded photograph inspected. It confirms the local carved frame and figures but is too small for confident close analysis of garment seams or jewellery construction. ASI’s written record provides the circa-sixth-century attribution. Obtain a higher-resolution detail before deriving individual ornament patterns. Photo reuse rights are not established; research use only pending clearance.

**R4: Nidhanpur copper plates.** Good direct material reference for an administrative prop, engraving and document form, but issued by Bhaskaravarman and not a surviving wardrobe reference. Do not give earlier kings Bhaskaravarman’s exact charter in a supposedly documentary scene. The local photo’s CC BY-SA conditions remain in the source register.

## Practical attire decisions

| Component | Evidence and limits | Production rule |
|---|---|---|
| Lower garment | A royal waistcloth is described on a fifth-century Gupta coin. No Varman garment survives here. | Retain wrapped lower garment as a comparative reconstruction. Exact length, pleats and border width are interpretive. |
| Upper cloth | No named king’s shoulder drape established by checked evidence. | Keep approved draped silhouette as the series convention; record it as a proposal. |
| Material | No fibre analysis of a royal garment is available. | Fine cotton or silk-like finish is plausible design language; do not label a weave as proven muga/pat/eri royal dress. |
| Textile motifs | Sculpted ornament does not demonstrate a particular woven pattern. | Keep borders restrained and simple; no modern gamosa or identifiable modern community textile as ancient evidence. |
| Gold necklaces, earrings and armlets | Comparative elite imagery supports jewellery broadly; no inventory for a specific Varman king. | Vary arrangement for recognition, not alleged rank. No king-by-king “official” necklace. |
| Girdle | Specific embossed belts in our sheets are invented. | Maintain the established shape family; label clasp designs as art. |
| Crown/diadem | Neither a low band nor a large crown is authenticated for these individuals. | Retain low diadem for continuity. No feathered Ahom crown or tall fantasy tower crown. |
| Hair and facial hair | No ruler-specific description established. | Individualise respectfully; keep chosen style constant across views. |
| Footwear | No individual evidence established. | Barefoot is the current sheet convention, not proof kings never wore shoes. Do not infer daily practice from sacred sculpture. |
| Armour | Nidhanpur v. 9 uses armour/strength wordplay for Balavarman; not a specification. | Court reference sheets stay in court dress. Design combat variants only after a separate weapon/armour study. |
| Religious marks | Personal affiliations mostly unresolved. | No automatic tilak, tripundra, Buddhist robe, deity pendant or sacred thread. This avoids invented specifics. |

The gold-rich silk finish of the approved images is a visual decision. It should not increase automatically with each generation: twelve successive kings do not imply twelve documented stages of costume evolution.

## Remaining material limitations

The research gives a workable period-inspired wardrobe system; it does not yield twelve authenticated wardrobes. Higher-resolution Da Parbatia details, the complete Dubi edition, direct Umachal and Barganga readings, and securely dated regional male sculptures would strengthen subsequent versions. Do not fill these gaps with later Madan Kamdev, Ahom, Koch, Mughal or modern ceremonial clothing without explicitly identifying the change of period.
