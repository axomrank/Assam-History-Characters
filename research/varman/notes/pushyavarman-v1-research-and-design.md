## Religion: what can be said
Pushyavarman’s personal religious sect is not established by the sources consulted here. Do not identify him as Buddhist, Shaiva or Vaishnava solely from the beliefs of later rulers, the broader region, or a similar name elsewhere.

The downloaded Gait 1906, printed pp. 22–24, reproduces Xuanzang’s seventh-century account: deva worship was prominent; Bhaskaravarman did not profess Buddhism but respected learned Buddhist monks. That is evidence concerning a much later ruler and a visitor’s account, not a census of religious practice and not proof about Pushyavarman. Respect for Buddhist scholarship does not itself imply conversion. Older translations and their categorical descriptions need critical treatment.

Jae-Eun Shin, “Changing Dynasties, Enduring Genealogy: A Critical Study on the Political Legitimation in Early Medieval Kamarupa,” Journal of Ancient Indian History XXVII, opening printed p. 173, notes that the ruling families’ origin and ethnic identities remain obscure and examines their Naraka genealogies as political legitimation. Opening passage consulted online: https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/attachments/20170209/d815b926/attachment.pdf . Divine genealogy cannot establish personal sect or facial anatomy.

Further research lead: Rashmi Rekha Bhuyan, “Religious Interaction in Early Medieval Kamarupa: An Insight into the Kalikapurana,” 2021, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/03769836211052098 . Abstract concerns Brahmanism–Buddhism interaction in a later textual setting; full article not consulted and should not be projected onto the fourth century.
