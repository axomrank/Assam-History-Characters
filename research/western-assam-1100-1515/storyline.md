# Western Assam, 1100–1515: from the end of Kamarupa to the rise of the Koch

Research dossier for filling the content gap between the Pala dynasty (ends c. 1100) and the Koch kingdom (Biswa Singha, 1515).
Written 2026-10-26. Companion files: `planned-scenes.csv` (27 proposed scene rows) and `sources.md`.

**How to read the labels.** Every claim carries one of: **Recorded** (an inscription, coin or contemporary chronicle says it),
**Reconstructed** (historians infer it from scattered evidence; dates are approximate), **Tradition** (later chronicles, local lore),
**Legend** (a story that explains something, not evidence of it). Confidence is high / medium / low.

**Honesty about the research.** This was assembled in one session from web summaries (mostly Wikipedia articles that cite Sarkar, Barpujari, Gait,
Neog, Nath and Baruah) plus targeted searches. The sources above them are the real authorities and should be checked before anything is published as
fact (see `sources.md`). Where those summaries disagreed or flagged "citation needed", this file says so.

---

## 1. The one-page version

1. **c. 1100–1140.** Bengal's Pala emperor Ramapala ends the local Pala line of Kamarupa. His governors (Timgyadeva, then Vaidyadeva) rule, and Vaidyadeva
   breaks away after Ramapala's son Kumarapala dies. After that the written record nearly goes dark for sixty years.
2. **1206.** The Turkic general Bakhtiyar Khalji, who had just taken Bihar and Nadia, marches into the Himalayas toward Tibet and loses almost his whole army.
   The ruler of Kamarupa had refused to join him and, by one account, cut his retreat. This is the first Muslim army to touch Assam, and it fails.
3. **1226–1228.** Bengal's rulers try again (Iwaz Khalji, then Nasiruddin Mahmud of Delhi). Nasiruddin kills the Kamarupa king and installs a tribute-paying
   successor, **Sandhya**. In 1228 a different people, the **Tai-Ahoms under Sukaphaa**, cross the Patkai hills into the far east of the valley. The two stories
   run side by side for the next 300 years.
4. **1257.** Sandhya (now independent) defeats Bengal's governor **Yuzbak** by using the monsoon floods and cutting supply lines, then moves his capital west to
   **Kamatapur**. Kamarupa becomes **Kamata**.
5. **1260–1400.** Kamata is a medium-sized Hindu kingdom between the Karatoya and the Barnadi rivers. It fights Bengal (raids in 1321–22, a deep invasion in
   1356–62, another in c. 1396–1400), fights and marries into the Ahom royal house, and produces the first Assamese literature. Local landholding chiefs, the
   **Baro-Bhuyans**, grow in power, helped by newcomers whom Kamata's kings settle near Guwahati and Bordowa.
6. **c. 1365–1440.** Bhuyan usurpers (Arimatta and successors) hold the throne. Then the **Khen dynasty** (Niladhwaj, Chakradhwaj, Nilambar, 1440–1498) rules from
   a great fortified city at Kamatapur (now Gosanimari).
7. **1492–1502.** Bengal's Sultan Alauddin Husain Shah conquers Kamata (Kamatapur falls c. 1498; Nilambar is imprisoned) and pushes as far as Hajo. His son Danyal governs.
8. **c. 1505–1515.** The Bhuyans, led by Harup Narayan, wipe out Danyal's garrison. A Mech-Koch chief's son, **Bisu**, learns warfare in this turmoil, defeats the
   Bhuyan chiefs one by one, and in **1515** is crowned **Biswa Singha**, founder of the Koch dynasty.

**The single idea that explains most of it:** after the Palas there was no strong centre in the Brahmaputra valley. Power sat with (a) a river-and-flood
landscape that defeated armies from the plains, (b) many local landholders (Bhuyans, tribal chiefs, Kachari and Chutia kings), and (c) a Bengal sultanate to the
west that kept trying to expand up the valley and kept getting stopped by geography until it finally took Kamata in 1498, and then could not hold it.

## 2. The map in words

- **Karatoya river** (west): the long-standing western edge of Kamata and the line the Bengal sultans fought over.
- **Kamatapur / Gosanimari** (near Cooch Behar): Kamata's capital after c. 1257.
- **Kamarupanagara / North Guwahati**: the earlier capital, abandoned after Yuzbak.
- **Manas, Barnadi** (east): later the Koch kingdom's boundary; east of the Manas the Kamata kings' control was loose.
- **Kopili valley, Nagaon, Bordowa, Langamaguri**: the Bhuyan heartland and the Kachari-Bhuyan-Kamata borderland. This is where Sankardev is born.
- **Dhansiri valley, Dimapur**: the Dimasa (Kachari) kingdom, with a brick-walled capital whose ruins survive.
- **Sadiya**: the Chutia kingdom's seat in the far east, tied to the goddess Kechai-khati.
- **Charaideo**: the Ahom capital, founded 1252.
- **Gaur / Lakhnauti** (west, in Bengal): the Sultanate's centre.

## 4. The story in seven chapters

### Chapter 1: After the Palas (c. 1100–1206): a sixty-year blank

- **Recorded (medium):** Ramapala of Bengal ends the Kamarupa Pala king Jayapala's rule c. 1100 (Sandhyakar Nandi's *Ramacharita*; supported by Vaidyadeva's Kamauli plates). **Timgyadeva** is made governor, rebels, and is replaced by **Vaidyadeva** (c. 1126–1140), who issues land grants in the old Kamarupa style (Kamauli plates, c. 1139–1142) and breaks away after Kumarapala's death. *(Those details are flagged "citation needed" in the Wikipedia summaries; they come from Choudhury, Sircar and Majumdar.)*
- **Reconstructed (low):** A "lunar dynasty" of local kings on the north bank (Bhaskara c. 1120–1200; Rayarideva; Udayakarna with an inscription of 1185; Vallabhadeva) and chiefs in the Sylhet region (Kharabana, Gokuladeva, Narayana, Kesavadeva). Scholars list names, not stories.
- **What it means:** Kamarupa breaks into small units. The old practice of granting police and revenue rights to local intermediaries leaves self-sufficient landed domains, which Amalendu Guha argues are the seed of the **Baro-Bhuyans** (13th c.).
- **The gap, honestly:** nothing reliable tells us what ordinary life looked like. Any scene here must be marked as an artistic reconstruction.

### Chapter 2: The Turkish raiders and the flood landscape (1206–1257)

- **1206, Bakhtiyar Khalji (Recorded, medium-high).** He leaves Devkot in March with a large force (traditionally 10,000 horsemen), guided by the Mech chief **Ali Mech**, following the Teesta/Sikkim route toward the Chumbi Valley, where Tibetan resistance and unfamiliar passes defeat him. On the retreat the army finds a stone bridge over the river broken, supplies cut, and starves (soldiers eat their horses). Only about 100 return (Minhaj's *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* as relayed by later historians). The **Rai of Kamrud** had refused to join him and, in Bhattacharya's account, cut his supply line. Bakhtiyar dies soon after: of illness, or killed by Ali Mardan Khalji (sources disagree).
  - *Identifying the king as "Prithu" (Tradition/Reconstructed, low):* the name comes through later tradition; its only early authority is the *Tabaqat-i Nasiri*, and the "Kanai-Barasi-Bowa" rock inscription popularly linked to this victory is not independently verified.
- **1226–1228, Iwaz Khalji (Recorded, medium).** The Bengal sultan reaches the Nagaon region and is defeated (Gachtal inscription); numismatic evidence suggests he reached Guwahati. He is then killed by **Nasiruddin Mahmud**, a Delhi prince.
- **1228, Nasiruddin Mahmud (Recorded, medium).** He crosses through Jalpaiguri into Kamrup. The king (Prithu in tradition) fortifies but is killed (an assassination plot is reported). Nasiruddin installs a successor who pays tribute: this is **Sandhya** (c. 1228). Nasiruddin dies 1229 and Bengal falls into confusion until c. 1251.
- **Meanwhile far to the east (Recorded, high):** in 1228 Sukaphaa's Tai party enters the valley.

### Chapter 3: Sandhya, the flood, and the move to Kamatapur (1229–1260)

- **Reconstructed (medium-low):** After Nasiruddin leaves, Sandhya stops paying tribute, pushes Muslim garrisons out, takes control up to the Karatoya, raids across it into Gaur and takes the title **Gaudesvara**.
- **1257, Yuzbak (Recorded in outline; details reconstructed; medium).** In early spring Malik Ikhtiyaruddin **Yuzbak**, Bengal's governor, invades. He crosses the Karatoya near Goraghat, marches through Goalpara, and occupies Kamarupanagara (Guwahati), which Sandhya had evacuated. Sandhya retreats to the hills; he is said to have dammed a hill stream and, when the spring rains came, released the water to flood the plain. His forces cut supply lines. A guide offered a shorter route through the foothills toward Devkot; the army was trapped in a narrow valley and destroyed. Yuzbak was captured (and executed, July 1257) or died of his wounds (sources conflict). He is said to have built the first mosque in Assam.
  - **Dispute:** Wikipedia's Sandhya article gives his reign as 1228–1260; the Kamata article dates the kingdom's start "c. 1257" and elsewhere Sandhya c. 1250–70. Treat as a range.
- **After 1257 (Reconstructed, medium-low):** Sandhya moves west to **Kamatapur** (hard to attack across rivers) and styles himself **Kamateswara**. Kamarupa becomes **Kamata**.

### Chapter 4: Kamata at its most active (1260–1400)

- **Rulers (Reconstructed, low):** Sindhu Rai (c. 1260–85), Rup Narayan (c. 1285–1300), Singhadhwaj (c. 1300–05); then the minister **Pratapdhwaj** seizes the throne (c. 1305–25); his cousin **Dharmanarayan** takes it and is challenged by Pratapdhwaj's son **Durlabhnarayan**; a settlement splits the realm (Durlabhnarayan keeps the core including Kamatapur, Dharmanarayan keeps Rangpur and Mymensingh).
- **1321–22, Bahadur Shah of Sonargaon raids as far as Nagaon.** Pratapdhwaj's resistance is ineffective; the **Kacharis** repel the sultan with heavy losses. Bahadur is killed 1327–28 by Muhammad Tughlaq.
- **Ahom connection (already in the site as AHOM-041 to AHOM-048):** the first Ahom–Kamata war ends in a stalemate and a royal marriage (Rajani/Bhajani, a Kamata princess, marries Sukhaangphaa). Her son **Chao Pulai** later flees to his uncle in Kamata after a failed plot against Sukhrangphaa. *Dating note: the site dates this war to c. 1305; the Wikipedia summaries and the site's own timeline event (event-1324-09) say c. 1324. Resolve before adding Ahom-side scenes.*
- **Culture (Recorded/Tradition, medium-low):** **Hema Saraswati** (*Prahlada Charita*, usually called the earliest known Assamese poem) and **Harihara Vipra** are court poets of Durlabhnarayan. **Madhav Kandali** composes the *Saptakanda Ramayana* "at the behest of Mahamanikya, Barāha Rājā" (the king's name is in his own Lankākānda), a ruler of the Nagaon-Morigaon-Hojai area. Literature in Assamese begins here, a century before Sankardev.
- **The Bhuyan newcomers (Reconstructed, low):** On Neog's reading, under a treaty between Dharmanarayan and Durlabhnarayan, **Chandivara**'s group (seven Kayastha and seven Brahmin families, traditionally from Kannauj) is settled at **Langamaguri** near Guwahati, later at Bordowa. Sankardev descends from this line. Amalendu Guha reads the Bhuyan rise differently (from local intermediaries). Both can be partly true.
- **1356–1362, Ilyas Shah and Sikandar Shah (Recorded in coins; medium).** Kamata has been weakened by Ahom and Kachari pressure; a minor Hindu chieftain had declared independence by 1329. Ilyas Shah of Bengal comes up the Brahmaputra, takes Guwahati, and dies c. Nov 1357; a coin of 1357–59 minted at "Chawlistan urf Kamrup" suggests occupation. Sikandar Shah continues; Mahamanikya, Indranarayan and Bhuyan chiefs organise resistance and fight him at **Gachtal near Nagaon** in 1362 (the outcome is not stated in the sources found).
- **c. 1365 onward:** **Arimatta**, a Bhuyan, overthrows Indranarayan; his successors (Gajanka, Sukranka, Mriganka) are weak (Reconstructed, low).
- **c. 1396–1400, Azam Shah (Recorded in coins; medium-low).** Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah of Bengal exploits the conflict between the Ahom king Sudangphaa and the Kamata raja; coins of 799 AH (1396–97, Koch Bihar) and 802 AH (1399–1400, Guwahati) show a Bengal presence. The Kamata ruler then allies with the Ahom king by marriage and together they expel the Sultan across the Karatoya (already on the site as AHOM-280).

### Chapter 5: The Khen kings and the great city (c. 1440–1498)

- **Origin (Tradition, low):** The Khen dynasty's founder **Niladhwaj** (1440–1460) was, according to the 18th-century *Gosani Mangala*, a poor boy named **Kanta Nath** from Taluk Jambari; others say a migrant from Tripura. The Khen were Tibeto-Burman in origin, ethnicity uncertain; possibly linked to the Khyen/Kheng peoples.
- **Rulers (Recorded in outline, medium):** Niladhwaj (1440–60), **Chakradhwaj** (1460–80), **Nilambar** (1480–98). Capital: **Kamatapur (Gosanimari)**, a huge fortified brick-and-earth city. Nilambar is described as the most powerful, ruling from the Karatoya to the Barnadi (a claim of wider conquests is uncited).
- **Culture (medium):** Court patronage of Brahmins and poets continues. **Sankardev** is born c. 1449 into a chief Baro-Bhuyan family at Alipukhuri near Bordowa (alternatively Patekibari near Bhuragaon). Traditional dates are 1449–1568; the historian Baniprasanna Misra argues for a birth between 1479 and 1484. Orphaned young, he studies under Mahendra Kandali (from about age 12). He builds a *devagriha* at Bordowa c. 1498.
- **East meanwhile:** the Chutia kingdom is at its height (inscriptions: Satyanarayana's 1392 Habung grant, Durlabhnarayana's 1428 grant); the Dimasa (Kachari) kingdom at Dimapur defeats an Ahom force c. 1490 (AHOM-082, AHOM-083).

### Chapter 6: The Sultan takes Kamatapur (1492–1502)

- **1492–93, Muzaffar Shah (Recorded, coins; medium):** the Bengal Sultan takes at least parts of Kamata and mints coins with the title **"Kamata Mardan"** (conqueror of Kamata).
- **1498, Alauddin Husain Shah (Recorded in outline; details Tradition):**
  - The traditional trigger: **Sachipatra**, a Brahmin whose son had been executed by Nilambar for an affair with the queen, instigates the invasion (Tradition, low; not firm evidence).
  - Shah Ismail Ghazi leads the force; the fort of Kamatapur is besieged and the city destroyed; Nilambar is imprisoned. The Khen dynasty ends. Dates conflict: siege from c. 1493; fall 1498; an expedition sent 1499; annexation up to **Hajo** completed by March 1502.
  - Husain Shah's coins call him "conqueror of Kamru, Kamata, Jajnagar and Urisa"; he inscribed the victory on a stone at Malda. His son **Shahzada Danyal** governs the conquered region from Hajo.
- **Disputed numbers:** an uncited infobox claims Kamata fielded 15,000 cavalry, 50,000 infantry, 200 ships and elephants against 20,000 cavalry, 40,000 infantry and 100 ships. Treat as unverified.

### Chapter 7: The Bhuyans, the Koch, and 1515 (c. 1505–1515)

- **c. 1505–1510 (Reconstructed, medium-low):** The Baro-Bhuyans, led by **Harup Narayan** (descended from Gandharva-raya of Bausi), attack Danyal's garrison and destroy it "to the last man" (Baruah). Dates vary: 1505 (Khen article) vs "within about ten years" of 1502.
- **The Koch rise (Reconstructed, medium):** Several Koch tribes had been united under a chief **Hajo** (Rangpur and Kamrup). His daughter Hira married the Mech chief **Hariya Mandal**; their son **Bisu** inherits Hajo's legacy and becomes eastern Koch chief at Khuntaghat. Around **1509** Bisu starts campaigning, allies with tribal chiefs and defeats the Bhuyans of Ouguri, Jhargaon, Karnapur, Phulaguri, Bijni and Pandunath (Guwahati), needing a stratagem against the Bhuyan of Karnapur. He may first have fought under the Bhuyans against the Sultan.
- **1515:** Bisu is crowned **Biswa Singha**, king of Kamata, between the Karatoya and the Barnadi, taking a Hindu name and (later Brahmin pandits say) a divine descent from Shiva. The capital moves Chikana, Hingulabas, then Kamatapur. His brother **Sisu** becomes yuvaraj. Hinduisation spreads slowly among the lower orders.
- **What the Bhuyans lose:** the Nagaon Bhuyans are squeezed between Kacharis and Koch, and in the early 16th century move north of the Brahmaputra. Sankardev, from this same circle, leaves Bordowa c. 1516 after a Kachari-Bhuyan conflict (Ekasarana biographies).

## 5. Four threads that make it easier to follow

1. **Geography beats armies.** In 1206, 1257 and 1357–62 the invaders are beaten less by an enemy battle line than by river crossings, monsoon floods, broken bridges and cut supply lines. This is the thread to emphasise in scenes.
2. **The Baro-Bhuyans are the connective tissue.** They are the landholders under every Kamata king, the base for Sankardev's family, the force that destroys Danyal, and the people Biswa Singha has to defeat. Whoever the king is, the Bhuyans decide how long he lasts.
3. **Kamata and Ahom are neighbours, not just enemies.** Wars (c. 1305/1324), a royal marriage (Rajani), an exile to the uncle's court (Chao Pulai), a joint defence against Azam Shah. The Ahom stories (AHOM-041 to 048, 053, 072, 237, 280) are the same story seen from the east.
4. **Early Assamese literature grows in Kamata's courts** (Hema Saraswati, Harihara Vipra, Madhav Kandali) before Sankardev, who is born into the Bhuyan world these courts supported.

## 7. Things not to say as fact (open disputes)

- The date Kamata begins: 1228 (Sandhya installed) vs c. 1257 (capital moved) vs c. 1250–70.
- The first Ahom–Kamata war: c. 1305 (the site's scenes) vs c. 1324 (the site's event and Wikipedia).
- Whether Bakhtiyar's Kamrup contact was with a king named **Prithu**, and the "Kanai-Barasi-Bowa" inscription's reading.
- Yuzbak: captured and executed (July 1257) or died of wounds.
- Husain Shah's campaign: 1492–93 start; 1498 fall; 1499 dispatch; 1502 completion.
- Danyal's end: 1505 or about ten years after 1502.
- Nilambar's fate after capture (imprisoned; nothing firm).
- The Sachipatra story (a Tradition explaining the invasion, not documented cause).
- Sankardev's birth: 1449 (traditional) vs 1479–84 (Misra).
- Chandivara's origin: Kannauj refugees (Neog) vs local intermediaries (Guha).
- "Baro" = twelve or "many".
- Biswa Singha's parentage: Mech father and Koch mother (Nath) vs either (Sarkar).
- Source quality: several summaries carry "citation needed" or are stubs; the army-size figures are uncited.

## 8. What a painter must not assume

- No Mughal domes, no Rajput forts, no European castles; this is brick-and-earth ramparts, moats, stilted timber houses, river boats, elephants, thatch and tile.
- Bengal Sultanate forces: Turkic and Afghan cavalry, but expect Bengali infantry and river craft; the early 13th-century raiders were small mounted forces; the 15th-century forces were large and river-borne.
- Kamatapur's walls were earth and brick; do not turn them into a stone citadel.
- Dimapur's famous ruins (stone pillars, brick walls) are a Kachari city; keep it separate from Kamata.
- Keep Hindu temple form out of Kamata royal houses and out of Ahom or Koch dwellings (see `research/ahom/agent-guidance.md`).
- Costume for the Bhuyans/Brahmin/Kayastha families can follow the project's Bhuyan direction in the same guidance file (plain white dhoti and shoulder cloth for adult men), as an artistic choice, not a historical claim.

## 10. Rivers as borders in this period

Kamata's western edge is the **Karatoya** and its eastern edge the **Barnadi** (loose beyond the **Manas**), under Sandhya's successors and the Khens alike; the
Sultanate pushed to Hajo and perhaps the Barnadi in 1498-1505; Biswa Singha's kingdom of 1515 kept the same Karatoya-Barnadi span. Further east the Dimasa held the
Dhansiri valley and the Chutias the Subansiri-Lohit-Dihing country. These are the `realm` rows in the data; see `research/places/river-boundaries.md` for every era,
the disputes and the sources.
